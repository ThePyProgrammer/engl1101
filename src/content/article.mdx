# What Does Respect<br/>*Sound* Like?

Here's how MARTA's David Banner campaign used sound to capture our collective responsibility to share Atlanta.

</Hero>

<Intro label="Ride with Respect">

The very first thing MARTA's newest Ride with Respect ad asks you to do is be annoyed.

And how couldn't you be? A rider's blasting music through the enclosed space of a bus, dancing to the tunes. Another is smoking. Someone's dropping trash behind. And yet, the bus churns forward. None of this is affecting its actual motion, but it sure makes the ride feel _a lot_ more chaotic.

Anyone who's taken the MARTA in the past recognizes this annoyance. The constant noise that plagues riders' journeys has been a mainstay for **years**. Perhaps even **decades**.

Just last week, my whole trip on the MARTA bus was spent trying to ignore ladies shouting at the top of their lungs across the bus. I witnessed someone dragging a large structure joined by ropes around the MARTA, the pieces grating against the hard bus floor.

Having grown up in Singapore for the majority of my life, where public transport tends to be quieter, Atlanta's MARTA has consistently irritated me, and I think most riders feel the same way. Sounds play a big part in our environments, and I believe that's exactly what MARTA was capitalizing on with this campaign.

<Section id="sound" label="Sound & shared space">

## Sound does most of the work.

MARTA's video starts with music that takes over the entire space. Unlike visuals that you can look away from, or conversations that you can avoid, opting out from someone's speaker isn't exactly something you can easily do in the narrow interior of a bus.

The awkward, exaggerated dancing across the narrow aisle makes that intrusion even more visible, letting viewers recognize the problem. It's clear: MARTA's riders often don't realize they're terrorizing a shared space.

MARTA's own Ride with Respect page puts this idea plainly: "MARTA belongs to all of us". And the video uses sound to show this to us.


</Section>

<Section id="banner" label="David Banner's entry" eyebrow="David Banner's entry">

## Then David Banner enters.

What MARTA interestingly does with this article is show the whole bus quiet down once David Banner, an influential American rapper, walks in. They employ a slightly pseudo-religious song to signify his arrival, portraying him partially as a godlike being that doesn't even need to use physical force to enact immediate correction to the disorder in the bus.

The audible "shh" made by Banner signifies his role as a "cultural elder" who uses his established community status to enforce authority, yet the use of the faint music makes his authority feel more righteous over bureaucratic. Effectively, it's like he's representing the "conscience" of the community that rides in MARTA buses every day.

MARTA could have delivered the same message through a station poster. Its website already tells riders to use headphones, avoid talking loudly, throw away trash, and show "basic civility". But this campaign shows us explictly what respectful behavior might look like with David Banner's voice.

MARTA has said publicly that it chose Banner for his "authenticity, strength, and credibility". Born in Mississippi, he has often described Atlanta as the city that adopted and embraced him. His place in the Southern hip-hop culture associated with Altanta gives that voice cultural weight, which he uses to restore order in the shared space.

Effectively, the campaign employs David Banner's cultural authority to put a voice to their code of conduct, and asks frequent MARTA riders to look around, because other people are there too.

</Section>

<Section id="city" label="A shared Atlanta">

## The Bus Becomes a Small Version of Atlanta

Atlanta is often advertised via skylines, stadiums, restaurants, music venues, and major events. Ride with Respect instead just shows us strangers sitting together on a city bus. In that way, it shows us a shared space in Atlanta, where people from different neighborhoods, routines, and communities are placed a few feet apart, sharing sound, movement, seating, and personal space. Hence, one rider's music or trash is immediately a part of another rider's experience of Atlanta.

The choice of using a bus as the environment is crucial as it shows an enclosed interior where every small choice makes a difference.

MARTA launched this campaign right after its public-awareness efforts during the 2026 FIFA World Cup. The agency has stated that the campaign will appear across its digital platforms, stations, trains, buses, and social media. In that way, riders are encouraged to watch this campaign in the exact space where these expectations apply.

There is also a limit to this version of Atlanta.

Ride with Respect focuses almost entirely on what riders can control. Delays, service frequency, and infrastructure also shape the everyday commute, but they fall outside its frame. In that way, MARTA is isolating the transit experience to that which passengers produce themselves: the actual social atmosphere.

A written code of conduct can tell riders what behavior is prohibited. This video asks them to notice what that behavior feels like to everyone else on board.

</Section>

<Closing>

## The bus keeps moving either way. The question is what kind of shared space moves with it.

And in MARTA's version of Atlanta, respect is not just something riders are asked to read on a sign. It is something they are supposed to hear, see, and practice together.
