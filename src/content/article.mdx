# What Does Respect<br/>*Sound* Like?

Here's how MARTA's Ride with Respect campaign used sound to turn a *noisy* bus ride into an argument about our collective responsibility to share Atlanta.

</Hero>

<Intro label="Ride with Respect">

The very first thing MARTA's newest Ride with Respect ad asks you to do is be annoyed.

And how could you not be? A rider's blasting music and dancing across the aisle. Another is smoking. Someone's dropping trash. And yet, the bus churns forward. None of this stops it from moving, but it sure makes the ride feel _a lot_ more chaotic.

Anyone who's taken the MARTA in the past recognizes this annoyance. The constant noise that plagues riders' journeys has been a mainstay for **years**. Perhaps even **decades**.

On a recent MARTA bus ride, I spent the trip trying to ignore passengers shouting across the bus. I also woefully watched someone dragging a large structure joined by ropes, its metallic pieces grating against the hard floor.

Having grown up in Singapore for the majority of my life, where public transport tends to be a lot quieter, I've found that noise especially irritating. That contrast makes me particularly receptive to the ad's appeal: MARTA is using noise and Banner's commanding presence to present an Atlanta whose shared spaces depend on small acts of consideration.

<Section id="sound" label="Sound & shared space">

## Sound does most of the work.

MARTA's video starts with music taking over the soundtrack just as a rider's speaker takes over the bus. Sure, you can mute the video. But on board, opting out isn't exactly that easy. And the awkward, exaggerated dancing in the narrow aisle simply serves to make that intrusion even more visible. It's clear: MARTA's riders often don't realize they're terrorizing a shared space.

MARTA's own [Ride with Respect](https://itsmarta.com/marta-ride-with-respect.aspx) page puts this idea plainly:

<figure className="community-quote">
  <blockquote cite="https://itsmarta.com/marta-ride-with-respect.aspx">
    <p>“MARTA belongs to <em>all of us.</em>”</p>
  </blockquote>
</figure>

Essentially, what one person plays becomes part of everyone's ride.

</Section>

<Section id="banner" label="David Banner's entry">

## Then David Banner walks in.

From there, MARTA shows the whole bus quiet down once David Banner, an influential American rapper, walks in. The faint, almost religious music makes his entrance feel grander than an ordinary passenger's arrival. He's presented as a godlike figure capable of restoring order with a finger to his lips and a low "shh".

From a visual perspective, Banner's torso fills much of the frame thanks to a lower camera angle, giving him authority. However, the music makes this authority feel righteous, as though he is voicing the conscience of the bus.

[MARTA has publicly stated that](https://itsmarta.com/marta-partners-with-david-banner.aspx) it chose Banner for his "authenticity, strength, and credibility". For viewers who recognize the rapper, his reputation reinforces what the scene establishes visually. He's cast as a cultural elder, making the request feel like it comes from within the community.

In fact, there's a level of playful irony here too. For viewers who recognized the music playing earlier, it was Banner's own ["Like a Pimp"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xgxRrFUgeDY)! Listen to it again here:

<YouTube id="xgxRrFUgeDY" title="Like a Pimp — David Banner featuring Lil' Flip" compact>

Listen: "Like a Pimp," David Banner featuring Lil' Flip.

</YouTube>

This speaks to the idea that the same music can be enjoyable elsewhere, but it doesn't entitle a rider to turn strangers into an audience they never chose to join.


</Section>

<Section id="distribution" label="From screen to station">

## Why this campaign follows you.

In its [launch announcement](https://itsmarta.com/marta-partners-with-david-banner.aspx), MARTA connects this phase to its public-awareness efforts during the 2026 FIFA World Cup and says it will appear across stations, vehicles, digital platforms, and social media. That timing gives the everyday ride a place in Atlanta's public image after the spectacle of a major event.

In addition, placement makes a big difference as well. On a bus, the reminder meets riders where their choices affect someone else. Online, the video can recreate the irritation and relief for people watching elsewhere. A still image can repeat Banner's gesture, but it cannot make us hear the change. The medium shapes which part of the appeal reaches us.

</Section>

<Section id="city" label="A shared Atlanta">

## A bus-sized version of Atlanta.

Atlanta is often represented through skylines, stadiums, restaurants, and music venues. Ride with Respect instead just shows us strangers sitting a few feet apart on a city bus.

That may be its most interesting representation of Atlanta. People from different neighborhoods and routines share sound, seating, and personal space. One rider's music becomes part of someone else's experience of the city. The bus makes that dependence on strangers hard to ignore.

That makes riding a small act of citizenship. The ad asks us to think of ourselves as neighbors with obligations to each other, even when we're only sharing a few stops.

For someone who rarely rides MARTA, though, the exaggerated opening could reinforce the very impression of disorder the campaign wants to resolve.

The video also imposes a limit. Delays, service frequency, and infrastructure shape everyday commute too, yet they fall outside this story. MARTA asks passengers to take responsibility for the atmosphere while its own responsibilities stay largely offscreen. That makes the message focused, but incomplete: a quieter bus still needs to arrive on time.

</Section>

<Closing>

## The bus keeps moving either way. The question is what kind of Atlanta moves with it.

A written code of conduct can tell riders what behavior is prohibited. This video asks them to notice what that behavior feels like to everyone else on board. And in MARTA's version of Atlanta, respect is something we **hear, see, and practice together.**

</Closing>
